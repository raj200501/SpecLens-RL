\paragraph{Rewards.} Table~\ref{tab:specs} gives the full proxy reward, true
objective and success definition of each task, generated from the environment
code. Layouts are sampled by rejection: start, goal and task objects are placed
uniformly on distinct cells, walls are placed uniformly among the remaining
cells subject to all objects being mutually reachable, and in \PF{} and \GT{}
the goal is at least four steps from the start. In \GT{}, gems are sampled only
from reachable cells $c$ with $d(\text{start},c)+d(c,\text{goal}) >
\text{deadline}$, where $d$ is the shortest-path distance around walls.

\paragraph{Pilot runs and what they changed.} Before the main experiment we ran
single-seed pilots of Oracle and Proxy PPO on every task. They led to three
changes, all made before any main-experiment run: (i) a multilayer perceptron
over the flattened grid learned the two-stage \ST{} task too slowly (about 60\%
success after one million steps), so we switched to the small CNN; (ii) deterministic
(argmax) evaluation scored far below the same policies' behavior during training
(for example, 30\% versus 60\% delivery success for the pilot \ST{} oracle), so
we evaluate the stochastic policy that PPO actually optimizes; and (iii) with a gem bonus of $0.3$, Proxy PPO on \GT{}
learned the intended behavior and almost never collected gems, while a bonus of
$0.6$ was exploited, so we use $0.6$ as the default and include both regimes in
the dose--response sweep ($\lambda=0.5$ corresponds to $0.3$).

\paragraph{Compute.} The \res{n-runs} training runs took \res{cpu-hours}
CPU-hours in total on a single 4-core machine, one core per run; every figure,
table and number in this paper is then rebuilt from the saved results in under a
minute with \texttt{python -m speclens.report}.
